### Analysis

Computer science’s roots are surprisingly ancient. The abacus and the Antikythera mechanism illustrate early attempts to mechanize calculation and simulate natural processes. Medieval scholars such as Al‑Khwarizmi and Fibonacci formalized numerical operations, providing a language for later algorithmic development. The 19th‑century vision of Babbage and Lovelace turned these ideas into tangible machines, establishing the core concepts of programmability and automation.

The 20th century forged the discipline into a rigorous science. Turing’s abstraction of computation introduced a universal model; von Neumann’s architecture provided a practical blueprint. Shannon’s theory of information enabled efficient data transmission, while Hopper’s compiler and Ritchie/Thompson’s UNIX/C system made programming accessible. Berners‑Lee’s invention of the Web transformed computation from a laboratory curiosity into a ubiquitous societal infrastructure.

Today, computer science permeates every domain: AI models predict disease progression, quantum processors promise exponential speedups, and the IoT connects sensors to cloud analytics, reshaping manufacturing, healthcare, and urban planning.
