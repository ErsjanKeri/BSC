# April 30, Per-expert async pipeline (compute_async_experts) and the publish-overwrite race

## Status of this entry

CURRENT (mechanism, fix, code in tree). The wall-clock numbers cited here are for a single 20-iteration repro session at one cgroup budget (7 G); they are stable within that session (CV 0.16%) but have not been cross-day reproduced and have not been measured at 8 G or 22 G. Treat the existence of `compute_async_experts` and the deadlock-fix story as load-bearing; treat the absolute timing as preliminary until the broader benchmark sweep on May 1 (entry below) is done.

## Motivation

Last canonical pipeline entry: April 22, multi-topic sweep with `--uring-pipeline-v3` (now `--uring-async-projection-overlap`) winning 5.2 to 7.1 percent over `--uring-overlap` (now `--uring-projection-overlap`) across both models and every budget. The intuitively-stronger design, per-(expert, projection) tags with eager dispatch instead of per-(upgate-pair, down) split-tag, was on paper expected to extend that win further. We had a v3 implementation that did per-(upgate-pair, down) tagging; the v4 implementation pushes to per-(expert, projection) granularity (12 tags = 4 experts x 3 projections rather than 8 tags = 4 experts x 2 logical phases).

Hypothesis going in: more fine-grained dispatch should keep more eligible matmuls in flight at any given moment, which should give the NVMe more time to deliver the remaining reads behind active compute. That hypothesis was empirically refuted in the May 1 entry (below), but the implementation work that precedes that refutation needs documenting first.

## Implementation summary

In `src/llama-moe-pipeline.cpp`, alongside `compute_async_projection_overlap` (the v3 path), we added `compute_async_experts` (v4). Activated by `--uring-async-experts`, mutually exclusive with the other pipeline variants. Differences from `compute_async_projection_overlap`:

1. **Tag scheme**: 12 io_uring tags allocated as `tag = expert * 3 + proj` where proj 0 = down, 1 = gate, 2 = up. All 12 reads submitted up front from thread 0 before the dispatch loop. (v3 uses 8 tags as 4 upgate-pairs plus 4 down-singles.)
2. **State machine**: thread 0 maintains `loaded_proj[expert][proj]`, `computed_up[expert]`, `computed_gate[expert]`, `computed_swiglu[expert]`, `computed_down[expert]`. The picker iterates experts 0..n_expert_used-1 and picks the first eligible action by phase priority: UP_MATMUL, GATE_MATMUL, DOWN_MATMUL, with the AND_FINISH variants (UP_AND_FINISH, GATE_AND_FINISH) folding swiglu and quantize inline when the second matmul of a (up, gate) pair runs.
3. **Per-expert intermediate buffers**: `ae_up_out_per_expert`, `ae_gate_out_per_expert`, `ae_act_per_expert`, each sized `n_expert_used * ffn_dim`. v3 reused single buffers because v3 always finishes one expert end-to-end before starting the next; v4 may interleave matmuls across experts (UP_MATMUL[0] then UP_MATMUL[1] before GATE_MATMUL[0] is loaded), so each expert needs its own slot.
4. **Bit-exactness vs v3**: per-expert weighted output goes to `expert_weighted_out[expert * n_embd + k]`, populated in dispatch order. After the dispatch loop, a final accumulation pass sums in canonical expert-id order 0..n_expert_used-1. This is the same buffer and accumulation order v3 uses, so the floating-point summation order is bit-identical. Verified by 30-token MD5 match against v3 across multiple cache configurations.

The implementation passed 30-token correctness on first try. The catch came at the 2000-token level, on the 20B model, under cgroup 7 G memory pressure.

## The intermittent deadlock

Symptom: when running the canonical 20B / 7 G / c250 / lfu-aging mult=3 / 2000 token / seed 42 workload, occasionally (roughly 30 to 40 percent of runs) the binary would hang indefinitely. No output, no error, no progress. SIGTERM at the harness 4-hour timeout was the only way to recover.

A short-prompt smoke run (30 tokens) never reproduced. Higher cgroup budgets did not reproduce. Lower cache sizes did not reproduce. The hang was specific to cgroup 7 G with a long generation, indicating something about sustained memory pressure plus the long tail of dispatch iterations.

Initial gdb attach to a hung process showed: 7 of 8 worker threads were stuck inside `moe_barrier`, spinning on `barrier_gen.load(acquire)` waiting for `barrier_gen` to advance. The 8th thread had already returned from `compute_fused_v4` (the C function name at the time, since renamed to `compute_async_experts`) and was elsewhere in the ggml graph executor. That immediately said: **a thread skipped a barrier**. The barrier-count invariant was broken; whoever was missing was never coming back, so the other 7 spun forever.

## Diagnostic infrastructure

To pinpoint *where* the missing thread skipped, we added two pieces of instrumentation to the moe pipeline shared state:

1. **Per-thread last-progress markers**, one slot per thread, written via the `AE_MARK(sh, ith, dbg_round)` macro at every meaningful execution point inside the dispatch loop and inside each switch-case body. The macro stores `(__LINE__, dbg_round)` to that thread's slot.
2. **30-second watchdog inside `moe_barrier`**: if the spin loop exceeds 30 seconds, abort with a diagnostic dump of all per-thread markers. The thread whose `(line, iter)` is *behind* the others identifies who got stuck and where they last made forward progress.

This converted "infinite hang" into "watchdog abort with stack trace and per-thread state dump" within 30 seconds, which made iteration tractable.

We also added `BSC_ASYNC_EXPERTS_DEBUG` (originally `BSC_V4_DEBUG`), an env-var debug knob that logs every picker decision and every barrier crossing on thread 0 to stderr.

## Three wrong hypotheses, all refuted by data

We did not find the right answer on the first pass; the rest of this section is what we tried and why each was wrong, because the correct fix is non-obvious and the wrong fixes are individually plausible.

### Hypothesis 1: torn read of a plain `int` field

The `ae_action_type` and `ae_action_expert` fields, written by thread 0 and read by all threads after the dispatch barrier, were originally plain `int`. With multiple decode steps reusing the same `moe_pipeline_shared`, a stale read of a previous step's value (sentinel -1, AE_DONE) was a possible explanation: a slow worker reads a value left over from a previous decode, sees AE_DONE, and breaks out of the dispatch loop while other threads are still inside.

Fix attempted: convert both fields to `std::atomic<int>` with `memory_order_seq_cst` on both stores and loads. This rules out torn reads and pins ordering to the atomic itself rather than relying on the surrounding barrier.

Result: gdb signature unchanged, deadlock still reproduced at the same rate.

### Hypothesis 2: barrier implementation bug

The barrier was originally `pthread_barrier_t`; commit `d7e480f4` (April 15) had switched to a custom atomic sense-reversing barrier to escape the libgomp futex path under contention. Maybe the new barrier had a subtle bug.

Fix attempted: revert to `#pragma omp barrier` for the barrier inside `compute_async_experts`, keep the atomic barrier elsewhere.

Result: same gdb signature. Different barrier implementations, same deadlock. The barrier itself was not the problem.

### Hypothesis 3: missing `pending_per_tag` reset

When the same shared state is reused across decode steps, if `pending_per_tag[]` from the previous step's wait_any_upgate_ready calls leaks into the next step, completion bookkeeping could miscount. Briefly investigated but ruled out: the shortcut path in `wait_any_upgate_ready` checks pending counters before drain, and the per-step counters are explicitly reset.

## The actual root cause: publish-overwrite race

After exhausting plausible single-bug hypotheses, we re-read the dispatch loop end-to-end against the gdb evidence. Looking at the order of operations:

```
iter K, thread 0: picks action_K, writes ae_action_type = action_K, enters barrier_K
iter K, all threads: cross barrier_K (whichever thread arrived last is the unique
                     last-arriver; it advances barrier_gen then returns immediately
                     while the other 7 are still spinning, see them via gen-bump,
                     return)
iter K, all threads: load ae_action_type, see action_K, execute switch body
iter K+1, thread 0: picks action_{K+1}, writes ae_action_type = action_{K+1},
                    enters barrier_{K+1}
```

The race window: after the LAST arriver of barrier_K stores `barrier_gen` and returns, but BEFORE that thread loads `ae_action_type`. If the OS scheduler preempts that thread in this tiny window (between barrier exit and the atomic load), thread 0 may have already finished its switch body, looped back, picked action_{K+1}, and written `ae_action_type = action_{K+1}` (overwriting action_K). When the preempted thread resumes and loads, it reads action_{K+1}, executes the wrong action's switch body.

Often that wrong action is AE_DONE (because thread 0 has finished its real work for the layer and picked DONE for the next iter). The misinformed worker breaks out of the dispatch loop. The remaining 7 workers, none of which broke, are now stuck at the barrier of iter K+1 forever waiting for the lost worker that already left.

`memory_order_seq_cst` does not save us: it guarantees each load returns *some* value the atomic ever had, but does not pin *which iteration's* value the load returns when the publisher races.

## The fix: two-barrier handshake

Convert "one barrier per dispatch iteration" into two barriers:

1. **Publish-visible barrier**: thread 0 has finished `picked_type/picked_expert`, written the atomic. All threads cross. After this barrier, all threads load the atomic.
2. **Read-complete barrier**: every thread has loaded the current iteration's value. Only after this barrier may thread 0 publish iter K+1's value.

```cpp
moe_barrier(sh, nth);                      // barrier 1
const int action_type = sh->ae_action_type.load(seq_cst);
const int E           = sh->ae_action_expert.load(seq_cst);
moe_barrier(sh, nth);                      // barrier 2
if (action_type == AE_DONE) break;
switch (action_type) { ... }
```

The second barrier closes the race window: thread 0 cannot pass barrier 2 until every other thread has reached barrier 2, which means every other thread has already executed the load of `ae_action_type`. After barrier 2, thread 0 is free to overwrite the atomic.

Cost: doubles the dispatch-iteration barrier count. We accepted that cost in this commit and quantified it the next day (May 1 entry below).

## Validation

Repro harness: 20 iterations of `pipeline_20b_7g_c250_lfua3_repro` under cgroup 7 G with the canonical 2000-token essay prompt (now known to early-stop at 838 tokens for 20B due to the model's self-limiting refusal, but bit-identically across all configs at seed 42).

Pre-fix: 20-iter repro in `cgroup_20260430_172907/`. 8 of 20 runs aborted via watchdog (40 percent failure rate), 12 of 20 completed with stable timings around 370 to 392 seconds eval. The watchdog dump shows the same per-thread mark pattern in every failed run: thread 0 ahead, one worker at a marker line that is several iterations behind the others. Mechanism confirmed.

Post-fix: 20-iter repro in `cgroup_20260501_140534/` (afternoon May 1 13:50 UTC, immediately after the two-barrier patch landed). 20 of 20 runs completed. eval_time_ms statistics:

| metric | value |
|---|---:|
| mean | 372062 ms |
| stdev | 608 ms |
| CV | 0.16% |
| min | 371044 ms |
| max | 373663 ms |

No watchdog firings. No stuck threads. Bit-exactness vs `--uring-async-projection-overlap` preserved.

## Critical caveats

These are NOT verified by this entry, in priority order:

1. **One budget only**. The 20-iter repro is at 7 G cgroup. The deadlock was specific to memory pressure; whether the fix is also necessary at 8 G, 9 G, or 22 G is theoretically yes (the race is structural and budget-independent in the code) but not empirically verified.
2. **One model only**. The 120B path uses the same code (modulo `n_expert_used`, `ffn_dim`), but the 120B sweep has not been re-run post-fix. There is no reason to think it would behave differently, but "no reason to think" is not the same as "verified".
3. **No isolation of the two-barrier cost**. We added two barriers per dispatch iteration without measuring how much wall-clock that itself costs. The May 1 entry below addresses this and its conclusions.
4. **No micro-benchmark of the race window**. We did not add a deliberate sleep between barrier exit and the atomic load to confirm the predicted reproduction frequency tracks delay. The mechanism is consistent with all observed evidence, but a stress test would add corroboration.
5. **The two-barrier handshake was REPLACED on May 1** by a 2-deep ring slot scheme (see May 1 entry). The fix described here is the one that closed the deadlock and was empirically validated in the 20-iter sweep above, but it is NOT what is in the tree as of May 1 evening. The replacement is also race-free, by a different mechanism, and bit-exact. The replacement was driven by the May 1 finding that the second barrier accounts for a large share of total barriers and is therefore worth eliminating if a race-free alternative exists.

## Wider implications

The deadlock diagnosis exposed a class of bug we had not been alert to: even with `seq_cst` atomics, a single-publisher pattern that races against a single barrier is unsafe if any reader can be preempted between barrier exit and atomic load. Our intuition that "atomic seq_cst is enough" came from textbook examples that assume per-iteration synchronization is one-to-one with the publication. As soon as the publisher can cycle around faster than the slowest reader can drain its read, the publication semantic is broken.

The general fix patterns are: (a) two-barrier handshake (used here in the immediate fix), (b) versioned slots indexed by iteration count modulo K, where K must be at least one greater than the maximum publisher-vs-reader spread (used in the May 1 redesign with K=2), or (c) producer-consumer queue with explicit hand-off. (a) is simplest. (b) is cheaper in steady state. (c) is the most general but the heaviest.

For the thesis: this work means we now have three pipeline variants (`--uring-projection-overlap`, `--uring-async-projection-overlap`, `--uring-async-experts`) all bit-exact at the output level under deterministic sampling, all running 20/20 without instability at the canonical 7 G workload. Whether async-experts is actually faster than async-projection-overlap is the question the May 1 entry investigates; spoiler: no, by a small structural margin.
