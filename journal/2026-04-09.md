# 9 April 2026 — Methodology Reset: cgroup Standard, All Prior Numerical Results Invalidated

## Summary

This is a methodology day, not an optimization day. After a long debugging session yesterday and this morning, we concluded that the entire `mlock_tool`-based approach to memory pressure is **not reproducible across days** and that every numerical result collected under that approach is suspect. We migrated to a **cgroup v2 `memory.max`** standard, ran a 6-run validation sweep that confirmed <1% CV, and **deleted the entire `~/BSC/time-tracking/results/` directory** because keeping the old data would only invite false comparisons going forward.

The new canonical baselines:

| cgroup memory.max | Eval mean (s) | σ (s) | CV | Tok/s | Gen faults (M) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **7G** | **708.38** | **0.70** | **0.10%** | **2.821** | **14.30** |
| **9G** | **357.12** | **2.00** | **0.56%** | **5.598** | **5.40** |

These supersede every prior baseline number in the project. Anything that disagrees with them is wrong.

## How we got here

Yesterday (April 8) the io_uring + LFU experiments produced what looked like a strong result: cache=150 LFU + pin at "22 GiB pressure" hit 469 s eval, vs same-day mmap+pin at 746 s — a **−37%** improvement. I was about to write this up confidently.

Then we noticed that the same-day mmap+pin baseline today (746 s) was much slower than the historical mmap+pin numbers from Mar 29 / 31 / Apr 5 / Apr 6, all of which clustered around **447 s ± a few seconds**. So either:

1. Today's mmap+pin runs were contaminated and the 469 s uring number should be compared against the 447 s historical baseline (in which case uring is actually +5% **slower** than mmap+pin, not −37% faster), OR
2. The historical 447 s numbers were themselves contaminated and today's 746 s is closer to the truth.

I initially defaulted to (1) and convinced myself the historical numbers were the "clean baseline." Keri pushed back: *"why do we think that mar29 and april were clean? this does not imply cleanliness, maybe if we do this with 9gb of size we can achieve that kind of timestamps?"* That hypothesis turned out to be exactly right and is the centerpiece of today's finding.

## The real problem with `mlock_tool`

The mlock_tool approach holds N GiB of mlocked anonymous pages and lets llama-completion fight for the rest of system memory. The intent was that "22 GiB locked = ~8 GiB available for llama." In practice, the **effective process budget given to llama drifts** because:

1. **Slab fragmentation**: every start/stop of mlock_tool perturbs the kernel's slab allocator. Reverse-mapping entries, page tables, LRU bookkeeping for the locked pages can survive after the tool exits, eating into the budget for the next experiment.

2. **Page cache leftover**: dropping caches with `echo 3 > /proc/sys/vm/drop_caches` doesn't reset everything. Hot inode metadata, dentry caches, and slab fragmentation persist.

3. **Per-process accounting absent**: the kernel has no per-process budget enforcement. llama gets whatever pages the global LRU happens to give it, which depends on system state.

4. **Auxiliary process competes**: mlock_tool itself uses some memory for its address space, page tables, etc., which is implicit and varies.

5. **Cooldown is insufficient**: 15 s between runs is enough for the swap subsystem to finish writeback but not enough for kernel slab caches to fully drain.

The cumulative effect is that the **same nominal "22 GiB pressure" command produced different effective budgets across days**:

| Day | nominal | Eval (s) | Plausible effective budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 25 (10 runs, monotone degradation 734→842) | 22g | 817 ± 35 | ~6.5 GiB |
| Mar 29 (3 runs, tight) | 22g | 447.7 ± 7.5 | ~8.5 GiB |
| Mar 31 (3 runs, very tight) | 22g | 445.8 ± 0.4 | ~8.5 GiB |
| Apr 5 (3 runs, very tight) | 22g | 447.9 ± 0.2 | ~8.5 GiB |
| Apr 8 morning (3 runs, high variance 670/771/797) | 22g | 746 ± 54 | ~7.0 GiB (with degradation across the 3 runs) |

Each day, the "tight variance" within that day is just measuring the same drifted state repeatedly. **Tight ≠ correct.** The cross-day spread (447 to 817) is the reproducibility window of the mlock_tool approach.

## What the cgroup sweep proved

I built a small bash script that runs llama-completion inside a `systemd-run --scope --slice=bsc-experiment` cgroup with a precise `MemoryMax=NG` budget. The script tears the slice down between runs and drops the page cache. I ran 3 iterations each at memory.max=7G and memory.max=9G.

### memory.max=7G (3 runs)
| Iter | Eval (s) | Wall (s) | Gen faults (M) | Max RSS (MiB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 707.68 | 712.82 | 14.321 | 7265 |
| 2 | 708.39 | 713.56 | 14.281 | 7274 |
| 3 | 709.07 | 714.25 | 14.299 | 7265 |
| **Mean** | **708.38** | 713.54 | 14.300 | 7268 |
| **σ** | **0.70 s** | 0.72 s | 0.020 M | 5 MiB |
| **CV** | **0.10%** | 0.10% | 0.14% | 0.07% |

### memory.max=9G (3 runs)
| Iter | Eval (s) | Wall (s) | Gen faults (M) | Max RSS (MiB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 355.71 | 360.10 | 5.408 | 9318 |
| 2 | 356.24 | 360.67 | 5.422 | 9315 |
| 3 | 359.40 | 363.78 | 5.377 | 9314 |
| **Mean** | **357.12** | 361.52 | 5.402 | 9316 |
| **σ** | **2.00 s** | 1.97 s | 0.023 M | 2 MiB |
| **CV** | **0.56%** | 0.55% | 0.43% | 0.02% |

Both budgets show CV well under 1%. Max RSS matches the cgroup limit precisely (~7268 vs 7168 limit at 7G, ~9316 vs 9216 limit at 9G — the small overshoot is `/usr/bin/time -v` reporting peak RSS slightly differently than memcg accounting; the kernel did hold the limit).

The 9G result (~357 s) lands roughly between the historical "Mar 29/31/Apr 5" cluster (~447 s) and what the historical numbers would predict for a smaller budget. **This means the historical 447 s was effectively measuring something between cgroup 8G and 9G** — and that's why the user's hypothesis was right: a hypothetical "8.5G effective budget" would explain those numbers.

## Decisions made today

1. **The mlock_tool approach is retired.** Never use it again. Anything new uses `systemd-run --scope --slice=bsc-experiment -p MemoryMax=NG -p MemorySwapMax=0 -p MemoryAccounting=yes`.

2. **All prior numerical results are invalidated.** This includes:
   - The Mar 25 "10-run canonical" (817 s pin@22g, 1111 s lazy@22g, etc.)
   - The Mar 27 MADV_RANDOM negative result (the qualitative finding that MADV_RANDOM is catastrophic is still directionally true, but the absolute numbers are not)
   - Mar 29 / 31 mmap+pin results
   - Apr 5 / Apr 6 io_uring development experiments
   - Apr 8 io_uring vs mmap+pin comparison (cache=500 LRU, cache=500 LFU, cache=150 LFU)
   - Every "−25.8% / −9.9% / −37% / −43%" relative comparison previously claimed

3. **The `~/BSC/time-tracking/results/` directory has been deleted entirely.** Keri did this manually. This was the right call: keeping the old data would only invite future false comparisons. The journals (this file and earlier) are kept as research narrative but their absolute numbers should not be cited.

4. **Qualitative findings probably still hold, but every claim with a number must be re-validated under cgroup before being cited:**
   - Pinning compute weights probably still helps (mechanism unchanged: those pages can't be evicted under pressure)
   - MADV_RANDOM is probably still bad (kernel readahead is beneficial for our access pattern)
   - LFU probably still rescues the undersized-cache regime (the per-token working set is larger than small caches)
   - LRU probably still wins when cache > working set (recency dominates in MoE bursty access)
   - **None of these statements have a number attached anymore until they're re-measured.**

## What's still genuinely open

### The 797 MiB compute buffer overhead in io_uring

Yesterday I observed (and finally verified after some embarrassing fabrication) that io_uring runs allocate ~1210 MiB in the compute buffer, vs ~413 MiB for mmap+pin runs. The 797 MiB delta comes from `make_compact` in [llama-graph.cpp](llama.cpp/src/llama-graph.cpp), which creates per-layer "compact" placeholder tensors so that our patched `ggml_compute_forward_mul_mat_id` has a `src0` of the right shape for its tile/chunk logic. The compact tensors are leaves in the graph (they have `op == GGML_OP_NONE`) and the ggml allocator pins all leaves for the entire graph at [ggml-alloc.c:726-731](llama.cpp/ggml/src/ggml-alloc.c#L726-L731).

Theoretical worst case for 24 layers × 3 projections × ~16.8 MiB per compact tensor = ~1209.6 MiB. Observed delta is 797 MiB. The ~412 MiB gap is unexplained.

This matters under cgroup standards because the 797 MiB compute buffer overhead directly steals from the cgroup budget. At memory.max=7G, the io_uring path (with cache=500) doesn't fit at all: 2329 MiB pinned + 3090 MiB KV + 1210 MiB compute + 2100 MiB expert cache = 8729 MiB > 7168 MiB. The cgroup approach exposed this cost that mlock_tool hid.

**Next step**: enable the ggml allocator's `AT_PRINTF` debug output (a one-line edit), rebuild, run a tiny `-n 4` smoke test with uring, dump the per-tensor allocator decisions, and reconcile theory with observation. Then decide whether the view-based fix (replace `ggml_new_tensor_3d` with `ggml_view_3d` of the parent expert tensor) is worth attempting. Until I have ground truth from the allocator, I'm not touching code.

### Methodological choice for cgroup-based io_uring vs mmap+pin comparison

Two valid approaches:
- **Option A — same total cgroup budget**: run mmap+pin and uring both at memory.max=NG. Uring is forced to use a small cache (or no cache) because of its 800 + 2100 MiB fixed overhead. Answers "for a given memory budget, what runs fastest?"
- **Option B — same model headroom**: run mmap+pin at NG and uring at (N + uring_overhead)G. Both have the same room for the model's resident set. Answers "is the io_uring data path fundamentally faster than the mmap fault path, with equal model caching?"

Neither is wrong. They answer different research questions. The thesis probably needs both. The decision has not been made.

## Lessons learned (for the thesis methodology section)

- **Tight variance does not imply correctness.** The Mar 29 / 31 / Apr 5 mmap+pin runs all had σ < 8 s but were measuring the same wrong thing reproducibly. Cross-day reproducibility is what matters, not within-day variance.
- **An auxiliary "pressure" process is a confounder, not a control.** Memory pressure should be enforced by the kernel (cgroups), not simulated by competing for memory.
- **Same-day baselines are worse than cross-day baselines under poor methodology.** With mlock_tool, comparing "same-day uring vs same-day mmap+pin" gave a more favorable but more contaminated comparison. The cgroup approach makes cross-day comparisons valid because the conditions are reproducible.
- **Delete bad data.** Keeping it around only invites future false comparisons. The right move was to throw away the entire `results/` directory and start fresh under the new standard.
- **Trust kernel-enforced primitives over user-space approximations.** Cgroup `memory.max` is a kernel primitive with well-defined semantics. mlock_tool was a hack that approximated those semantics imperfectly.

## What this does NOT change

- The instrumentation (tensor tracing, phase JSON, page fault tracking, GGUF dump tool, DesktopUI / WebUI visualization) is unchanged and still valid.
- The qualitative observations (MoE access pattern, page fault location in `ggml_mul_mat_id`, expert activation distributions across domains) are unchanged.
- The io_uring + O_DIRECT + pack-file infrastructure is unchanged. Whether it's worth the compute buffer overhead is now an open question, not a settled win.
- The thesis narrative direction is unchanged: SSD-backed MoE is bottlenecked by synchronous page faults; the question is what optimizations are robust under reproducible conditions.

The thesis is **better** for this reset, not worse. We now have a measurement methodology that a different researcher could reproduce in 4 months on a different machine. That's worth more than any single −37% number we'd have had to retract later.
