# May 1, Why is async-experts slower than async-projection-overlap? Profiling sprint and the structural ceiling

## Status of this entry

CURRENT (mechanism, code, instrumentation). The conclusion is a **negative result**: async-experts cannot be made faster than async-projection-overlap on this hardware/workload without redesigning either the synchronization model (background completion thread) or the prefetch model (cross-layer expert speculation), neither of which is in the BSC scope. The wall-clock numbers cited here are session-local (one cgroup setup, one workload, one machine). Cross-day reproducibility was not done.

## Motivation

April 22 closed with a clear thesis claim: `--uring-pipeline-v3` (now `--uring-async-projection-overlap`) wins 5.2 to 7.1 percent over `--uring-overlap` (now `--uring-projection-overlap`) across both models and every budget. The natural follow-up question was: does pushing finer (per-(expert, projection) tags, eager dispatch, the v4 path implemented April 30 above) extend the win? On paper the answer is yes: more dispatch granularity should let the NVMe stay saturated longer and hide more I/O latency behind compute.

The data refused this. In every run, async-experts measures slightly **slower** than async-projection-overlap. Initial hand-wave readings of pipelining.md showed a 6.4 second slower number in a 367.7 second eval (about 1.7 percent), which is small but real and stable across iterations. The user pointed out that we did not yet know what was inside that 6.4 second number, and asked for a profiling-driven investigation rather than guesses. The rest of this entry is what that investigation found.

This entry also covers the housekeeping done at the start of the day: renames and dead-code removal that put the codebase in the state used for the rest of the investigation.

## Establishing the gap with measurement, not memory

200-token canonical workload, cgroup 7 G, c250 lfu-aging mult=3, eager-compute, pin, seed 42. Two runs back to back, drop_caches and 5-second cooldown between:

| metric | apo (async-projection-overlap) | ae (async-experts) | delta (ae - apo) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| elapsed (s) | 44.548 | 44.928 | +0.380 |
| task-clock (ms) | 295496 | 298705 | +3209 |
| user (s) | 281.92 | 284.81 | +2.89 |
| sys (s) | 14.07 | 14.35 | +0.27 |
| cycles (G) | 1510 | 1524 | +14.4 |
| instructions (G) | 1091 | 1101 | +9.93 |
| IPC | 0.72 | 0.72 | identical |
| I/O bytes | 165 GB | 165 GB | bit-identical |
| cache hits/misses | 19776/37548 | 19776/37548 | bit-identical |

The user-CPU delta divided by 8 worker threads (+0.36 s/thread) lands within run-to-run noise of the elapsed delta (+0.38 s). That tells us the extra work is **distributed across all worker threads**, not concentrated on thread 0. So the gap is not "thread 0 does some heavy thing while others wait"; it is "every thread does a bit more work than in the apo path".

`perf stat -d -d -d` over the same workload also rules out the obvious causes:

| | apo | ae | comment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| L1d miss rate | 2.10% | 2.15% | within noise |
| L1i miss rate | 0.09% | 0.10% | within noise |
| dTLB / iTLB misses | similar | similar | not the bottleneck |
| branch mispredict rate | 1.33% | 1.32% | identical |
| context switches | 267967 | 267697 | identical |
| page faults | 1288392 | 1290035 | +1643, matches `bsc_phases_json` total fault delta |

Same IPC, same memory hierarchy hit rates, same branch behavior. ae is doing more of the same thing as apo, not different things.

## Round 1: flame-graph attribution. Two surprises.

`perf record --call-graph dwarf` over the same 200-token runs, `perf script` then folded then symbolized. Top symbols by self-time:

| symbol | apo % | ae % | apo Gcycles | ae Gcycles | delta (Gcycles) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `moe_barrier(moe_pipeline_shared*, int)` | 28.53 | 28.56 | 451.7 | 457.9 | +6.18 |
| `__tls_get_addr@plt` | 26.07 | 26.13 | 412.7 | 418.9 | +6.18 |
| `ggml_vec_dot_f16` | 21.14 | 21.02 | 334.7 | 337.0 | +2.29 |
| `ggml_vec_dot_mxfp4_q8_0` | 14.39 | 14.15 | 227.8 | 226.9 | -0.98 |

Two surprises:

**Surprise 1: `moe_barrier` is 28.5% of CPU cycles in BOTH variants.** More than a quarter of the entire run is in the spin loop of the barrier. The question of "is this the bottleneck" answers itself.

**Surprise 2: `__tls_get_addr@plt` is 26%.** That is a function call (PLT trampoline plus TLS descriptor lookup), which has no business being on any hot path. Disassembly of `moe_barrier` (offset `0x11f301` in `libllama.so`) shows the call is INSIDE the spin loop, called once per iteration. The smoking gun: `static thread_local uint32_t spin_check = 0;` (a watchdog throttle counter) was being resolved via `__tls_get_addr@plt` on every spin iteration in -fPIC libllama.so.

Together, `moe_barrier` plus its TLS callout amount to roughly 54 percent of total CPU cycles in both variants.

## Round 2: spin_check stack-local fix. Negative wall-clock result.

Hypothesis: removing `__tls_get_addr@plt` from the spin loop should reduce CPU work and maybe wall-clock.

Fix: replace `static thread_local uint32_t spin_check = 0;` with a stack-local `uint32_t spin_check = 0;`. Watchdog semantics preserved (the counter only needs to throttle clock_gettime calls within one barrier wait; reset-on-entry is fine). One-line change at `llama-moe-pipeline.cpp:260`. Bit-exactness preserved across both variants (4-way MD5 match before and after).

Disassembly confirms the fix: hot spin loop drops from 10 instructions plus a function call to 7 register-only instructions. `__tls_get_addr@plt` is gone from the moe_barrier callgraph.

Result on perf-stat at the same workload:

| metric | apo pre | apo post | delta apo | ae pre | ae post | delta ae |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| elapsed (s) | 44.548 | 44.580 | +0.03 | 44.928 | 44.955 | +0.03 |
| task-clock (ms) | 295496 | 296196 | +700 | 298705 | 299371 | +666 |
| cycles (G) | 1510 | 1517 | +7 | 1524 | 1532 | +8 |
| **instructions (G)** | **1091** | **882** | **-208** | **1101** | **882** | **-218** |
| **branches (G)** | **131.7** | **73.1** | **-58.6** | **133.0** | **73.9** | **-59.1** |
| L1-dcache loads (G) | 334.7 | 209.7 | -125 | 327.4 | 210.6 | -116 |
| IPC | 0.72 | 0.58 | | 0.72 | 0.58 | |

The fix did everything it was supposed to do at the instruction level: 19 percent fewer instructions retired, 44 percent fewer branches, 37 percent fewer L1-dcache loads. But cycles were unchanged. **Wall-clock was unchanged** (+0.03 to +0.11 s, within noise).

Mechanism: the spin loop is dominated by the `pause` instruction's stall cycles, not by the user-mode instructions inside it. Removing the TLS call removed work that was being retired, but did not remove time. IPC dropped because we now have fewer instructions filling the same number of cycles, most of which are pause stalls anyway.

The fix stays in the tree, because the new code is cleaner and correct, but it does not move wall-clock. **First negative result.**

## Round 3: parity-slot to eliminate the second barrier. Negative wall-clock result.

Hypothesis: the gap is the BARRIER COUNT, not per-barrier wait. ae has more dispatch barriers than apo because of the two-barrier handshake added on April 30 (publish-visible plus read-complete). If we can eliminate the second barrier without reintroducing the publish-overwrite race, ae's barrier count drops below apo's, and ae should win.

Fix: 2-deep ring slot for `ae_action_type[2]` and `ae_action_expert[2]`, indexed by `iter & 1`. With one barrier per iteration the spread between any two threads' iteration counts is bounded by 1, so two slots is provably sufficient: iter K writes slot K&1, iter K+1 writes slot (K+1)&1 (different slot), iter K+2 reuses slot K&1 but by then the slowest reader has already crossed barrier_{K+2} and loaded its iter K+1 value. Race-free. Bit-exact preserved (4-way MD5 match again).

Result with new instrumentation `moe_barrier_count` and `moe_barrier_wait_ms` (sum of per-thread spin time across all waiters at all barriers):

| metric | apo | ae pre-fix | ae post-fix | delta ae (pre to post) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `moe_barrier_count` | 100317 | 148087 | **85986** | **-62101 (-42%)** |
| `moe_barrier_wait_ms` (sum) | 159545 | 162156 | 162827 | +672 |
| avg wait per waiter per barrier (ms) | 0.227 | 0.156 | 0.270 | doubled |
| elapsed (s) | 44.398 | 44.928 | 44.955 | +0.027 |

The fix did exactly what it was supposed to: dropped barrier count by 13 per layer call (62,400 across 200 tokens, measured 62,101). ae's barrier count is now BELOW apo's. **Yet ae is still 557 ms slower** vs apo in this run.

Mechanism: total barrier wait time is ESSENTIALLY CONSERVED. With fewer barriers but bigger compute chunks between them, each barrier sees more arrival skew, so per-barrier wait roughly doubled (0.156 to 0.270 ms). The product `count * wait/barrier` is nearly invariant. We pushed the cost from one place to another without reducing it.

This is the **conservation law** that made the rest of the investigation hard: total barrier wait time is a function of total cross-thread imbalance, which is independent of how the barriers are placed. Granularity changes do not move the total.

Fix stays in the tree (correct, cleaner, fewer atomic operations on shared cache lines). **Second negative result.**

## Round 4: parallel block-aligned quantize. Negative wall-clock result.

Hypothesis: the AND_FINISH actions (UP_AND_FINISH, GATE_AND_FINISH) end with a serial Q8_0 quantize step that only thread 0 runs. Threads 1 to 7 sit at the next dispatch barrier waiting for thread 0 to finish quantize. Parallelizing the quantize should reduce that arrival skew.

Fix: each thread quantizes its own block-aligned slice of the activation vector into the corresponding block-aligned slice of the output buffer. Q8_0 has 32-element blocks, each independent (own scale, own int8 quants), so this is bit-exact. For ffn_dim 2880 = 32 × 90, the 90 blocks distribute as 12/12/11/11/11/11/11/11 across 8 threads. Block-aligned distribution differs from the matmul/swiglu's `ffn_dim/nth` distribution, so the internal barrier between swiglu and quantize stays (thread X reads act_e ranges originally written by other threads).

Result:

| metric | apo | ae | delta |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| moe_barrier_count | 100317 | 85986 | -14331 |
| moe_barrier_wait_ms | 160093 | 163250 | +3157 |
| elapsed (s) | 44.588 | 45.090 | +0.501 |
| cycles (G) | 1517 | 1535 | +18 |

No change in barrier_wait_ms vs the previous round (162827 to 163250 within noise). No change in elapsed within noise. The serial quantize was not the source of the arrival skew; parallelizing it did not move the conservation law.

Fix stays in the tree (parallel work is more honest than serial work, and the change is bit-exact). **Third negative result.**

## Round 5: picker decomposition. The actual signal.

At this point three rounds of intra-iteration optimizations had not moved the conservation law. The next question was: where exactly does thread 0 spend its pre-barrier time? If it is in compute (state machine), we can optimize the picker. If it is in I/O wait, the gap is structural.

Instrumentation: two atomic counters in `moe_pipeline_shared`, written only by thread 0:

- `ae_t0_pick_compute_ns`: cumulative time thread 0 spends in pre-barrier work EXCLUDING blocking I/O.
- `ae_t0_pick_io_wait_ns`: cumulative time thread 0 spends inside `wait_any_upgate_ready` (synchronous block waiting for io_uring CQE).

Per-iteration timestamps with `clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC)` bracket the pre-barrier window; the I/O wait is measured separately around the wait_any call. Both surfaced via the `bsc_phases_json` line at run end as `ae_t0_pick_compute_ms` and `ae_t0_pick_io_wait_ms`.

200-token canonical run:

| counter | apo | ae | comment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `ae_t0_pick_compute_ms` | 0.00 | **9.86** | apo has no picker; ae's picker compute is essentially zero |
| `ae_t0_pick_io_wait_ms` | 0.00 | **16349.74** | ae's thread 0 spends 16.35 SECONDS blocked on wait_any_upgate_ready |
| `uring_phase2_wait_ms` | 15915.74 | 16344.96 | apo's analogous synchronous I/O wait (after each expert's compute, in `wait_expert_tagged`) |

The picker compute is negligible: 9.86 ms over 200 tokens, about 0.05 ms per layer call. NOT the bottleneck.

**Thread 0 spends 16.35 seconds blocked on `wait_any_upgate_ready` over a 45-second run.** While thread 0 sleeps in that call, 7 worker threads sit at the next dispatch barrier doing nothing.

Cross-check with the conservation law: 16349.74 ms × 7 waiters = 114443 ms, which is 70 percent of ae's total `moe_barrier_wait_ms` of 163016 ms. The "where is the time" question is answered by this single arithmetic identity.

apo is NOT immune to this pattern: its `uring_phase2_wait_ms` of 15916 ms is the analogous synchronous I/O wait inside `wait_expert_tagged`. Both variants pay synchronous-I/O-wait-by-thread-0. **The difference is +434 ms in ae**, which × 7 waiters = 3038 ms barrier wait (matches the measured +2923 ms `moe_barrier_wait_ms` gap within noise). Identity closed.

## Why ae's I/O wait is +434 ms higher than apo's

This is the actual mechanism, derived from data and structure rather than guessed:

apo's per-phase compute chunks are LARGE: "all 8 threads do one matmul of expert e" at a deterministic graph point. By the time apo calls `wait_expert_tagged(e)` for expert e's down read after running all of expert e's up + gate + swiglu + (serial) quantize, the down read submitted at layer entry has had several phases of compute time to finish. The wait is short, sometimes zero.

ae's dispatch chunks are SMALLER: each is one matmul of one expert. When the picker runs out of currently-eligible actions and calls `wait_any_upgate_ready`, less aggregate compute has elapsed than at apo's equivalent point. The kernel CQE may not yet be ready. Thread 0 sleeps. Threads 1 to 7 sit at the barrier.

The conservation law that we kept hitting (`moe_barrier_wait_ms` ~ 160K regardless of barrier count, regardless of parallel quantize) is exactly this: **the total synchronous I/O wait by thread 0 is a function of `(disk read latency) - (parallel compute that overlaps it)`**, and it does not change when we shuffle barriers around or parallelize quantize. We cannot change disk read latency. We can only change compute coverage. Smaller dispatch chunks give I/O less compute to hide behind; this is what ae trades to get its finer dispatch.

## What it would take to actually close the gap

Each option below was considered and is documented for completeness; none is in the BSC scope.

1. **Background completion thread**. A 9th thread that polls io_uring CQEs and updates `loaded_proj[][]` asynchronously. Removes thread 0's blocking entirely. Substantial concurrency redesign with race-prone interactions; adds a thread outside ggml's worker pool.
2. **Cross-layer prefetching**. Submit layer N+1's reads during layer N's compute. Requires expert speculation: the routing of layer N+1 is not known until layer N completes. `pipelining.md` explicitly flags this as out of scope.
3. **Drain all pending CQEs in `wait_any_upgate_ready`**. Current implementation returns one CQE per call. Draining all available could reduce the number of `wait_any` calls, but does NOT eliminate the FIRST-WAIT block: the disk still has to deliver the first read before any compute can begin. Marginal improvement, would not move the structural ceiling.
4. **Larger dispatch chunks in ae**. Defeats the design.
5. **Reduce moe_barrier overhead more aggressively**. The spin loop is already 7 register-only instructions plus pause; the atomic operations on `barrier_count` and `barrier_gen` are unavoidable for correctness. Removing the watchdog (saves the cold-path `clock_gettime`, gains a rare-instruction-count win, loses the deadlock-detection guard). Not worth it.

## Conclusion

The data forces a clean, defensible negative result: **ae's "single picker plus synchronous wait_any" design has a built-in approximately 1 percent wall-clock disadvantage vs apo on this hardware and workload**. The dispatch granularity benefit (more parallelism opportunity) is exactly cancelled by the synchronization granularity cost (more wait_any blocking points giving I/O less compute time to hide behind).

This refutes the going-in hypothesis "more fine-grained dispatch should be strictly better". The thesis can present this as a measured negative result with a clear mechanism (the conservation law on barrier wait time and the +434 ms thread-0-blocking identity).

For the optimization sprint itself: every fix landed today is correct, bit-exact (verified at 30 tokens with 4-way MD5), and reduces code complexity (fewer barriers, fewer instructions, parallel where serial was, cleaner spin loop). None moves wall-clock by more than run-to-run noise. The instrumentation added today is permanent and useful: `ae_t0_pick_compute_ms` and `ae_t0_pick_io_wait_ms` give a clean attribution split for any future investigation, and `moe_barrier_count` plus `moe_barrier_wait_ms` close the conservation-law observation.

## Critical caveats

In rough priority order, NOT verified by this entry:

1. **One day, one machine, one workload**. All numbers are from a single afternoon session on the TUM hiwi-02 server, 200-token decode, 7 G cgroup, c250 lfu-aging mult=3. The April 9 / April 14 methodology required cross-day reproducibility; we have not done that for this gap. The 1 percent number is likely stable but not validated.
2. **One model**. 20B only. 120B has the same code path; whether the conservation law holds at 120B's larger compute-per-action is plausible but not verified.
3. **One budget**. 7 G only. Whether the gap shrinks at 9 G (where there is more compute time per token) or at 22 G (where there is no I/O at all) is not measured.
4. **Drain-all CQEs not tested**. Item 3 in the "what it would take" list. If this turns out to give a real win, the conclusion above is too pessimistic.
5. **Picker compute does include the wait_any preamble** (eligibility loop, watch_tags assembly). That overhead is included in `ae_t0_pick_compute_ms` but the 9.86 ms total establishes it is not a contributor.
6. **No isolation of the spin_check fix vs the parity-slot fix vs the parallel-quantize fix on the conservation law**. We applied them sequentially and observed the conservation hold throughout. Reverting one at a time and re-running was not done; the conservation law might have a contributor that one of these masked.
7. **Cross-day stability of the 16.35 sec thread-0 I/O wait**. We measured it once. Disk timing varies. The exact number could shift; the 70 percent attribution is the load-bearing claim, and it is internally consistent (matches the moe_barrier_wait_ms identity within sampling noise).

## Implications for the thesis

The April 22 result (`--uring-async-projection-overlap` beats `--uring-projection-overlap` by 5.2 to 7.1 percent) remains the headline. It is NOT a stepping stone toward an even bigger async-experts win; on this hardware, async-projection-overlap is the local optimum for the synchronous-picker design family. async-experts is a sibling design with the same wall-clock and slightly less I/O-hiding, not a strict improvement.

Defensible thesis framing: "We implemented two distinct async-pipeline designs (per-(upgate-pair, down) split-tag, and per-(expert, projection) eager-dispatch). Both are bit-exact. The split-tag design wins by 5.2 to 7.1 percent over the synchronous-projection-overlap baseline. The finer-grained eager-dispatch design wins approximately equally over the baseline but does not extend the win, due to a measurable conservation law on synchronous I/O wait that finer dispatch does not avoid. Closing this gap further requires either background completion polling or cross-layer prefetching, neither of which was in scope."

That is a clean, mechanism-grounded, evidence-supported finding. It is a more nuanced story than "v4 is faster than v3"; it is also more honest.
